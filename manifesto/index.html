Biology is not a language

Today's models approximate biology from the data they were fed, and collapse the moment they meet something they have never seen. The answer is not a bigger model. It is to find the physics the natural world already encoded.

We are building biological intelligence: models grounded in physics that learn the true functions of living systems, so we can simulate life rather than approximate it, from a single interaction up to the whole cell.

We started with the microbial dark matter, the 99% of microbial life that has been sequenced but never grown. The 1% we can grow already remade medicine, agriculture, and industry; reaching even a few percent more would be one of the largest expansions of useful biology in a century. So we set out to predict what these organisms do, and how to grow them, using the best genome and protein models the field has produced.

They did not succeed. Within the distribution on which they were trained, the models are fluent; confronted with a genuinely unseen organism, which is the entire purpose of the exercise, they collapse. They do not generalize, and after extensive checking we concluded that the failure was not ours but structural: these models learn to sample from the data they were given, and the biology worth finding is, by definition, absent from it.

This essay sets out the argument that followed: that the failure is not one of scale, that biological data will never be sufficient to overcome it, and that the way forward is not to approximate biology from observations but to recover the function that governs how living systems move and interact.

The unseen is not the unknowable.

So we put the field's strongest models to work, and they failed.

To predict how a protein behaves, or whether two molecules will interact, one turns to the strongest models the field has produced: the large genome models and the protein language models. We used them. On proteins and interactions resembling those in their training sets, the results are impressive; on genuinely novel ones, the cases of interest, they degrade sharply. The predictions are not marginally worse. They bear no relation to reality once the input departs the distribution the model was trained on.

The cause merits precision, because it is not a matter of tuning. Nearly every model in this field is built on attention, sometimes combined with convolution or a regression head. Attention learns the geometry of its training distribution and samples from it: within that distribution it interpolates well, but outside it there is nothing to draw upon. Yet moving outside the distribution is precisely the objective. Biotechnology firms, biomanufacturers, and agricultural laboratories are all searching for a protein, an enzyme, or an interaction that has never been measured. These models are least reliable in exactly the setting that motivates their use.

the model is least reliable where it is most needed

Nor can scale resolve this. Scaling laws hold for transformers trained on text, but the Chinchilla results already show the returns diminishing, and biology has no comparable corpus to scale on. Sequenced life is scarce and costly to obtain: every structure, every measured interaction, every cultured organism represents months of laboratory work, and no expenditure converts biological data into a language model's corpus. Scaling is the most expensive route available here, and it terminates at the same limit.

So we stepped back and asked what such a model is actually doing.

A neural network approximates a function from observations. Consider the implication. If a network only ever approximates the function latent in a dataset, it retains an irreducible error, some epsilon, because no dataset can contain every case a function produces. A function maps infinitely many inputs, and no finite set of measurements can determine all of them. Driving that error to zero would require unbounded data, and in biology, where data is the scarcest resource, that is not a viable path.

So here is the alternative: rather than approximate the function, find it.

The immediate objection is that the space of candidate functions is infinite, and the search therefore intractable. It is not. The manner in which proteins move and interact is not a distinct law for each molecule; a single set of rules underlies all of them, much as there is one Schrödinger equation rather than one per atom. Approximating that law demands unbounded data to reach zero error; recovering it yields zero error by construction. And it is demonstrable in the ordinary manner, by carrying out the work, as the Schrödinger equation was. This is why it does not ultimately matter that we have not catalogued every protein.

Science already writes functions of this kind. The Navier–Stokes equations for fluids. The diffusion equation. The wave equation. The Gaussian. Euler's exponential. The logistic map. Each is a compact rule that generates unbounded specific behaviour, and none was obtained by enumerating every case it governs. Our claim is that biological interaction admits such a function, and that it can be recovered rather than fit.

Molecular dynamics is one means of evaluating that law, and a stronger approximator than any statistical model, because it integrates known physics rather than searching an unbounded space of candidates. Yet what the field commonly terms a “physics-based model” is frequently not physics at all. Simulating a small molecule or a single protein is straightforward; simulating a vaccine, an antigen engaging an entire cell, or a viral process traversing a membrane is not. Each would require a hand-specified force field, and constructing one is exceptionally difficult.

It also raises a question the field has not answered cleanly. At what level should the physics be approximated by a network: quantum, molecular, or mechanical? And what representation adequately describes a whole cell: protein, RNA, genome, or atoms? We do not yet know which data belongs to which model.

This is the point we return to. The natural world already encodes its physics within its data. If a network is intended to approximate the function of a protein, it should not require that physics to be supplied by hand, because the physics is already present in what we measure. The failure of current models is that they discard it at the first step, when a molecule is reduced to a token and only the order of the symbols is retained. Preserve the physical state in place of the token, and the law becomes recoverable. The search space is not infinite, and there are principled ways to constrain it.

❧ ❦ ❧

Recover the function and biology's hardest cases cease to be out of distribution, because there is no distribution to fall outside of. There is a law, and one evaluates it. The microbial dark matter that led us here becomes tractable, as does everything above it, from proteins to the interactions that become medicine.

Much of biology is more difficult than it appears, and this programme may fail. But the claim is testable in the most direct way: recover a function, evaluate it on biology no one has yet measured, and determine whether it holds. Even partial success alters what is reachable.

that is the bet

In a few years we expect to be writing the retrospective, reporting either why the approach failed and what it taught us, or a function we recovered, and the biology it predicted that had not yet been measured. Either way, the attempt is warranted.

On the Title

Today's models read biology the way they read text, as a distribution of tokens to be sampled. That is the mistake the title names. Biology is not a language; it is a physical system with functions underneath the data, and those functions are what we mean to find.
