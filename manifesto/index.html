Biology is not a language

Today's models approximate biology from the data they were fed, and collapse the moment they meet something they have never seen. The answer is not a bigger model. It is to find the physics the natural world already encoded.

We are building biology models that find the functions of life, starting with the microbial dark matter that showed us why approximation fails.

We started with the microbial dark matter, the 99% of microbial life that has been sequenced but never grown. The 1% we can grow already remade medicine, agriculture, and industry; reaching even a few percent more would be one of the largest expansions of useful biology in a century. So we set out to predict what these organisms do, and how to grow them, using the best genome and protein models the field has produced.

They did not work. Inside the distribution they were trained on, the models are fluent. Faced with a genuinely unseen organism, which is the entire point of the exercise, they collapse. They do not generalize, and after a great deal of checking we concluded the failure was not ours but theirs: these models learn to sample from the data they were fed, and the biology worth finding is, by definition, not in it.

This essay is the argument that followed. That the failure is not a matter of scale, that biology's data will never be large enough to fix it, and that the way out is to stop approximating the function of life from examples and to find the function itself.

❧ ❦ ❧

Of every hundred microbes on Earth, we have grown one. There is a bacterium in your gut right now, possibly several thousand species, that we have never been able to grow in a lab.

We know they exist. We have sequenced their DNA from your stool, from soil, from seawater, from hot springs. They sit in databases as ghost organisms: full genomes with no organism behind them. We can read every gene they have. We just cannot culture and study them.

this is true for 99% of microbial life on earth

Brute-force culturing has failed for a century despite genuine effort. The standard procedure is depressing: take a sample, dilute it, plate it on agar, try every growth medium you can think of, wait days to weeks, see what grows. Most labs run this with perhaps five media. The most ambitious labs run two hundred. The result is always the same: a handful of fast-growing generalists colonise the plates, and everything interesting stays invisible.

Ghost organisms: a complete genome, sitting in a public database, with no living cell behind it.

Some of what we are missing. Christensenella, the genus most strongly associated with leanness in human gut microbiome studies, of which we have grown less than one percent.1 Patescibacteria, an entire bacterial superphylum that lives by attaching to the surface of other bacteria, discovered through metagenomic sequencing in 2015, almost none cultured, their lifestyle so unusual that nobody is confident standard methods will ever work on them.2 Asgard archaea, the closest living relatives of the cell type that became all complex life. The first one, Prometheoarchaeum syntrophicum, was finally cultured in 2019 after twelve years of trying with a single sample.3 And the deep biosphere, bacteria living kilometres below the Earth's surface, in rock pores, with doubling times measured in thousands of years: sequenced, but not a single one grown.4

These are not edge cases. These are the rule. The cultivable microbes (the E. coli, the Bacillus, the lactobacilli upon which we have built a hundred years of microbiology) are weird, fast-growing outliers that happened to tolerate the conditions a human in a lab coat decided were "standard."

❧ ❦ ❧

Why does this matter?

Because every working antibiotic we have was discovered from cultivable microbes. Penicillin came from Penicillium, vancomycin from Streptomyces, a hundred others from a handful of soil genera. The drug discovery pipeline is running dry. We have squeezed the cultivable one percent for everything they have, and resistance is winning. The next generation of antibiotics, almost by definition, has to come from the uncultured 99%.

Note in the margin: we can see the genes for the antibiotics. We just cannot grow the microbes that make them.

We already know the uncultured majority makes novel drugs. We can read the genes, we just cannot grow the maker. Metagenomic mining (reading the DNA without growing the organism) has shown they encode biosynthetic pathways for hundreds of thousands of small molecules we have never characterised.5 The genes for the antibiotics are right there in the sequence. The cells that make them will not grow in a flask.

The next blockbuster probiotic almost certainly already exists as an unculturable sequence in a database. The next generation of microbiome therapeutics (drugs based on actual gut bacteria, not yogurt) is blocked by cultivability. Seres Therapeutics brought an FDA-approved microbiome drug to market;6 their pipeline is constrained by what they can grow. The next Akkermansia (itself only cultured in 2004, and now a billion-dollar market opportunity7) is waiting in someone's dataset.

The same is true for environmental remediation. Microbes that eat plastic, microbes that fix nitrogen without fertiliser, microbes that capture carbon. The actual best ones, the ones evolution spent four billion years optimising, are mostly uncultured.

And it is not only industry that depends on this. Unknown microbes remain a mystery in both the human body and the environment. Recent work has linked oral bacteria to Alzheimer's risk, gut bacteria to cognitive function, and extremophiles to unusually efficient metabolism, yet for most species we still do not know what they do. Microbial communities are central to health, but the majority remain functionally unmapped.

The unseen is not the unknowable.

So we tried to reach them, and the tools broke.

To predict what an uncultured organism needs, or what its proteins do, you reach for the best models the field has: the large genome models, the protein language models. We used them. On organisms resembling the ones in their training sets, they are impressive. On the uncultured majority, the ones we actually wanted, they came apart. The predictions were not a little worse. They were unrelated to reality the moment the input left the distribution the model had seen.

It is worth being exact about the cause, because it is not a tuning problem. Almost every model in this field is built on attention, sometimes with a convolution or a regressor attached. Attention learns the shape of its training distribution and samples from it. Inside that distribution it interpolates beautifully. Step outside, and there is nothing to draw on. But stepping outside is the whole job. The biotech companies, the biomanufacturers, the agricultural labs are all looking for a protein, an enzyme, an interaction that has never been measured. These models are useless in exactly the situation that pays for them.

the model is weakest exactly where it is needed

And scaling will not save it. Scaling laws hold for transformers on text, but the Chinchilla work already showed the returns bending down, and biology has no text to scale on. Sequenced life is scarce and expensive. Every structure, every measured interaction, every grown organism costs months of work, and no amount of money turns biology's data into a language model's corpus. Scaling is the single most expensive path available here, and it ends at the same wall.

So we stopped, and asked what the model is really supposed to be doing.

A neural network approximates a function from examples. Follow that to its end. If all a network ever does is approximate the function hidden inside a dataset, it carries an irreducible error, some epsilon, because no dataset can hold every case a function produces. A function maps infinitely many inputs; a finite pile of measurements can never pin all of them down. To drive that error to zero you would need infinite data. In biology, where data is the scarcest thing we have, that is not a plan.

So here is the better idea: instead of approximating the function, find it.

The reflex objection is that the space of possible functions is infinite, so the search is hopeless. It is not. The way proteins move and interact is not a fresh law for every molecule; there is one set of rules beneath all of them, the way there is one Schrödinger equation and not one per atom. Approximating that law needs infinite data to reach zero error. Finding it means zero error from the first line. And it is provable in the ordinary way, by doing the work, the way Schrödinger's equation was. Which is why it does not really matter that we have not catalogued every protein.

Science already writes functions like this down. Navier and Stokes for fluids. The diffusion equation. The wave equation. The Gaussian. Euler's exponential. The logistic map. Each is a small rule that generates endless specific behaviour, and none of them was found by collecting every case it governs. We are claiming that biological interaction has such a function, and that it can be found rather than fit.

Molecular dynamics is one way to run that law, and a better approximator than any statistical model, because it integrates known physics instead of searching an infinite space of guesses. But what the field usually calls a “physics-based model” is often not physics at all. It is easy to simulate a small molecule or a single protein. It is another thing entirely to simulate a vaccine, an antigen meeting a whole cell, a viral process crossing a membrane. Someone has to write a force field for all of that, and that is brutally hard.

It also forces a question no one answers cleanly. What level of physics are we approximating with a network at all: quantum, molecular, mechanical? And what data even describes a whole cell: protein, RNA, genome, atoms? We do not yet know which data belongs to which model.

Here is the part we keep returning to. The natural world already carries its physics inside its data. If a network is meant to approximate the function of a protein, it should not need us to hand it the physics; the physics is already there in what we measure. The failure of today's models is that they throw it away at the very first step, when they turn a molecule into a token and keep only the order of the letters. Keep the physical state instead of the token, and the law is recoverable. The search space is not infinite, and there are ways to make it smaller.

❧ ❦ ❧

Find the function and biology's hardest cases stop being out of distribution, because there is no distribution to fall out of. There is a law, and you evaluate it. The microbial dark matter that sent us here becomes reachable, and so does everything stacked above it, from proteins to the interactions that become medicine.

Most of biology is harder than it looks, and this could fail too. But the claim is testable in the plainest way: find a function, evaluate it on biology no one has measured, and see whether it holds. Even partial success changes what is reachable.

that is the bet

In a few years, hopefully, we will be writing the postmortem on this. Either "here is why it failed and what we learned," or "here is a function we found, and the biology it predicted that no one had yet measured." Either way, somebody has to try.

We are looking for more researchers in biophysics, machine learning, and molecular biology. And if you would like to help in any other way (wet-lab partnership, advisor role, environmental samples from weird places, or simply an idea), please reach out.
Miyu Horiuchi

On the Title

Today's models read biology the way they read text, as a distribution of tokens to be sampled. That is the mistake the title names. Biology is not a language; it is a physical system with functions underneath the data, and those functions are what we mean to find.
